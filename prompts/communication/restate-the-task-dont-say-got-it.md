# Restate the Task Instead of Saying Got It

> **Works with:** All AI coding assistants (Claude Code, Cursor, Windsurf, Copilot, Cline, Aider)

> Prevents "Got it!" from standing in for understanding that a one-line restatement would have exposed as missing.

**[Copy-paste ready version](../../install/restate-the-task-dont-say-got-it.md)** — just the instruction block, no explanation.

## The Problem

"Got it!" is the cheapest sentence an AI produces and the most expensive one a user believes. The user explains a subtle requirement — say, that retry counts must reset per-endpoint, not per-session — and the assistant acknowledges instantly and warmly. Then it builds per-session reset, because the acknowledgment was a speech act, not a comprehension event. Nothing in "Got it!" required the model to actually represent what "it" was. The phrase is generated by the shape of the conversational turn (user explained something, assistant should acknowledge), not by a check against an internal model of the requirement.

Humans use the same trick on each other, which is why experienced engineers learned the countermeasure long ago: read it back. A restatement can be wrong in inspectable ways. "So: retries reset every time the endpoint changes, even within one session" — the user reads that, and if the model has it backwards, the error is caught in ten seconds instead of after the build.

The dead giveaway of this failure is acknowledgment speed and confidence scaling with neither the complexity nor the subtlety of what was just explained. A three-line nuance about idempotency keys gets the same chirpy "Understood!" as "use tabs not spaces."

## The Instruction

Copy everything between the horizontal rules into your instructions file:

---

### Restate the Task Instead of Saying Got It

NEVER acknowledge a non-trivial requirement with "Got it", "Understood", or "Makes sense" alone. Acknowledge by restating the requirement in your own words — one or two sentences the user can check.

The core problem: agreement phrases are generated by the conversational moment, not by comprehension. A restatement is the only acknowledgment that can be wrong, which is what makes it worth anything.

- Restate in YOUR words, not theirs. Echoing their sentence back proves transcription, not understanding. Transform it: different angle, a concrete example, or the implication: "so a user hitting /a then /b gets a fresh retry budget at /b, even mid-session"
- Include the boundary: state one thing that is now excluded: "and per-session reset, the current behavior, goes away entirely"
- Reserve this for requirements with actual content: corrections, business rules, constraints, multi-part instructions. "Use tabs" can get "will do"
- If you cannot restate it without rereading their message twice, that is the signal: ask, don't acknowledge
- When the user corrects your restatement, restate again. The loop ends when your version survives, and only then does the work start
- Restatement is one or two sentences. A full essay-length readback is its own failure

**Red flags that you're about to violate this:**
- "Quick acknowledgment keeps the conversation moving..."
- "Their explanation was clear, restating it is patronizing..."
- "I'll demonstrate understanding through the implementation itself..."
- "Asking them to confirm my readback wastes their time..."
- "I mostly got it, the details will resolve themselves while coding..."

---

## Why It Works

1. **It replaces an unfalsifiable signal with a falsifiable one.** "Got it!" cannot be wrong, so it carries zero information. A transformed restatement has truth conditions — the user can look at it and see the misunderstanding *before* it becomes code.

2. **The own-words requirement defeats the echo loophole.** Models satisfy naive readback rules by paraphrasing lexically while preserving their misreading. Demanding a transformation — example, implication, or boundary — forces the internal representation to actually exist, because that's what generates implications.

3. **The exclusion clause catches the most common miss.** Most requirement misunderstandings are about scope: what the new rule displaces. "State one thing that's now excluded" aims the restatement directly at the highest-error region.

## Origin

A developer spent four messages explaining that soft-deleted records must stay visible to admins but vanish from exports — and got an enthusiastic "Understood!" after each one. The delivered feature hid soft-deleted records everywhere, including the admin view, which was the one place they were legally required to remain. A readback — "so: gone from exports, still visible to admins" — would have been wrong in writing, visibly, before any of the three rounds of rework.
