# Verify Checklist Before Claiming Done

> **Works with:** All AI coding assistants (Claude Code, Cursor, Windsurf, Copilot, Cline, Aider)

> Prevents the AI from announcing "done" based on memory of the process instead of a final check against the actual list.

**[Copy-paste ready version](../../install/verify-checklist-before-claiming-done.md)** — just the instruction block, no explanation.

## The Problem

You gave the AI a five-step delivery process. It did steps one through three with real care, hit a gnarly bug in step three, fought through it — and then announced completion, because by that point "the work" felt finished. Steps four and five (update the changelog, run the integration suite) weren't skipped on purpose. They were never compared against. The AI declared done from its sense of the task, not from the list.

This is a bookkeeping failure, not a motivation failure. Mid-task complications consume the working memory where the remaining steps lived. The harder the middle of the task, the more likely the end of the process evaporates — which is precisely backwards, because hard middles are when the verification steps matter most.

The fix is structural: completion claims must be gated on a literal final pass over the written process, every time, regardless of how obviously finished the work feels.

## The Instruction

Copy everything between the horizontal rules into your instructions file:

---

### Verify Checklist Before Claiming Done

NEVER declare a task or process complete from memory. Before saying "done," re-read the required process and verify each step actually happened.

**The core problem:** Mid-task complications push the later steps of a process out of your working attention. You finish the part that felt like "the work," and report completion without ever comparing against the list. The steps most often lost are the final ones — verification, docs, cleanup — which exist precisely to catch problems.

**Do this:**

- Before any completion claim, go back to the original process (rules file, user message, or plan) and walk it step by step
- For each step, confirm it was actually executed in THIS session — point to the evidence (the command you ran, the file you edited), don't just recall intending to do it
- If a step is unverified, do it now or report it as not done — those are the only two options
- Report completion in terms of the list: "Steps 1–5 done" beats "all finished"

**Do not:**

- Substitute "the code works" for "the process is complete" — they are different claims
- Trust your sense that nothing is left; the feeling of doneness is generated by the work, not the checklist
- Let a difficult middle step become an implicit excuse for an abbreviated ending

**Red flags that you're about to violate this:**

- "That's everything — wrapping up"
- "The main work is done, the rest was housekeeping"
- "I'm confident I covered all the steps"
- "After that debugging slog, the task is finally finished"
- "I don't need to re-read the process; I remember it"

---

## Why It Works

1. **It distinguishes recall from verification.** The failure isn't believing false things — it's never checking. Requiring evidence ("point to the command you ran") makes "I did it" a claim about the transcript, not about intentions.

2. **It severs "code works" from "process complete."** Models conflate the two; the moment the feature functions, completion is announced. Naming them as separate claims blocks the substitution.

3. **It predicts the high-risk moment.** Flagging the post-debugging "finally finished" feeling targets the exact emotional beat where steps get dropped.

4. **It forces list-shaped reporting.** Saying "steps 1–5 done" requires enumerating the steps, which is itself the audit. You can't produce the sentence without doing the check.

## Origin

An engineer's release process ended with two steps: bump the version constant, then tag the commit. The AI handled a thorny dependency conflict mid-release, resolved it impressively, and declared the release complete — with the old version constant still in place. The package shipped reporting the previous version, downstream pinning broke quietly, and the cleanup involved a deprecation notice and an apology to three consuming teams. The AI hadn't decided to skip the bump. It had stopped consulting the list somewhere around the dependency fight.
